How do I calculate my sweat rate?

Weigh yourself right before and right after a run of about an hour, in the same dry kit, and add back what you drank. The weight you lost plus what you drank, divided by the hours you ran, is your sweat rate (1 kg is about 1 liter). Endurance athletes average 1.28 L an hour, but with a spread of ± 0.57 L/h, and heat training can raise it, so measure your own in conditions like your race.

## Why weigh yourself?

It's the simplest accurate method. The [ College of Sports Medicine position stand (Sawka et al. 2007)](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597) says sweat rates vary widely between people, so drinking plans should be individual, and that weighing before and after exercise gives you an estimate.

A review of testing methods, [Baker 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0691-5), calls body mass change the simplest and most accurate way to get whole-body sweat rate, if you correct for non-sweat losses and sweat trapped in clothing. Intensity, weather, heat acclimation, fitness and body size all change the result, so one test is a snapshot. Baker works at the Gatorade Sports Science Institute (PepsiCo, which sells sports drinks), and the review has no new data.

## How do you do the test?

1. Go to the bathroom, then weigh yourself with no clothes or in dry, light kit.
2. Run or ride for about an hour at a steady effort you'd use in your race. Note everything you drink.
3. Towel off, take off sweaty clothes (or wear the same dry kit as before) and weigh yourself again before you pee or drink.
4. Sweat lost = weight before minus weight after, plus what you drank. Divide by the hours of exercise.

For example, you weigh 70.0 kg (154.3 lb) before a 1-hour run and 68.9 kg (151.9 lb) after, and you drank 0.4 L (about 14 oz). You lost 1.1 kg, plus 0.4 L drunk, so your sweat rate is about 1.5 L (about 51 oz) an hour. The 1 kg to 1 liter step is our conversion, close enough for training.

## How much do endurance athletes sweat?

About 1.3 L an hour on average, with a wide spread. [Barnes et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2019.1633159) pooled sweat testing data from 1,303 athletes tested from 2000 to 2017. Endurance athletes averaged 1.28 ± 0.57 L/h. American football players averaged 1.51 L/h, basketball 0.95, soccer 0.94 and baseball 0.83.

The ± 0.57 matters more than the average. It means plenty of endurance athletes sweat well under or well over the average, so a plan built on the average fits few people. These were athletes who came in for testing, not a random sample, and all the authors work at the Gatorade Sports Science Institute (PepsiCo).

## Does heat change your sweat rate?

Yes, and that's why one test in cool weather isn't enough for a hot race. In [Klous et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtherbio.2020.102697), 8 people cycled in the heat (33°C, or 91°F, and 65% humidity) for 10 days in a row. By day 10, local sweat rate had risen 58% on the arm and 36% on the back. Sweat sodium fell to about 60% of day 1. Sodium dropped from day 3, but sweat rate only rose from day 7 to 8. It's a small lab study with local sweat patches.

A randomized crossover trial agrees. In [Keefe et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.90119.2026), 18 highly trained runners (8 men, 10 women) did 7 sessions of 60 minutes in 40°C (104°F) heat and 7 matched sessions at 22°C. The heat block raised sweat rate more than the temperate one, and women and men adapted the same way. The abstract gives no effect sizes.

So a test taken before a heat block likely underestimates your sweat rate on a hot race day and overstates its sodium. Our article on [how long heat acclimation takes](https://raceproof.ai/blog/heat-acclimation-how-long) covers the heat block itself.

## What should you do with the number?

Use it as a ceiling, not a target. The [hyponatremia consensus (Hew-Butler et al. 2015)](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0000000000000221), from 17 experts, says low blood sodium during exercise mainly comes from drinking more than you sweat, and that drinking when thirsty is the safest individual strategy.

At the 2002 Boston Marathon, [Almond et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa043901) found low blood sodium in 13% of 488 runners who gave a usable blood sample. It was linked to gaining weight during the race (odds ratio 4.2) and to finishing in over 4:00 against under 3:30 (odds ratio 7.4). It's observational, so a link, not proof.

Replacing every drop doesn't seem to pay off either. A meta-analysis of randomized trials, [Zhang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1968824), found that a planned drinking schedule cut body mass loss by 1.47 percentage points against drinking to thirst, with no clear performance gain (0.7%, with a range from minus 4.4% to plus 6.2%). The certainty was very low, with 225 people across 24 studies.

## What it means for your race

**Marathon.** Know your sweat rate before you plan your bottles and aid stations. Never finish heavier than you started. In Boston, drinking more than 3 L and drinking every mile were each linked to low blood sodium ([Almond et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa043901)). If you have a heart or kidney condition or take medication that affects fluid or sodium, talk to your doctor before you set a drinking plan.

**Ultra.** At the Western States 100, [Hoffman & Stuempfle 2015](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000599) found low blood sodium in 6.6% of finishers, and none of them had lost more than 4.3% of body weight. The authors point to overdrinking, not too little salt. It's observational data from one race.

**Triathlon.** Test on the bike and on the run, because intensity changes sweat rate ([Baker 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0691-5)). Do one weigh-in around a long ride and one around a long run.

**When to start.** Measure your sweat rate now, then re-test in race-like heat within the heat window, 6 to 2 weeks before your race. These are general guidelines. [See your own timeline: try the demo](https://raceproof.ai/demo).

## Our take

*This is our opinion, not a study finding.* One weigh-in is a start, not an answer. Do three or more across easy and hard sessions and different temperatures, and redo one in race-like heat after your heat block. Then plan to drink less than you sweat: Raceproof's drinking plans replace about 50 to 80% of sweat loss, with thirst as your guide. Weigh-ins tell you about fluid only. Sodium needs a sweat test.

### Can I work out my sodium losses from a weigh-in?
No. A weigh-in gives fluid only. Sweat sodium needs a proper sweat test, and the result depends on how the sweat is collected and handled ([Baker 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0691-5)).

### Should I drink back everything I sweat?
No. Drinking more than you sweat is the main cause of low blood sodium in exercise ([Hew-Butler et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0000000000000221)), and a planned schedule showed no clear speed gain over drinking to thirst ([Zhang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1968824)).

### Do women and men adapt to heat the same way?
In one small trial, yes: 10 women and 8 men raised their sweat rate in the same way after a 7-session heat block ([Keefe et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.90119.2026)).
