How many carbs per hour should I eat in an ultramarathon?

<!-- answer -->
Guidelines put races longer than about 2.5 to 3 hours at up to 90 g of carbohydrate an hour, from a glucose and fructose mix. Most ultrarunners take less than that, and gut trouble is the bigger risk: at the Western States 100, 35.6% of runners who dropped out said gut symptoms were one reason. Intakes of 120 g/h have only been tested in small groups of trained or elite athletes, and in a mountain marathon they didn't change race time.

<!-- body -->
## What do the guidelines say?

The 2016 joint position statement from the [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and the American College of Sports Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26917108/) sets up to 90 g/h for exercise lasting more than 2.5 to 3 hours. [Jeukendrup 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-014-0148-z) puts ultra-endurance at about 90 g/h, which has to come from mixed sugars such as glucose plus fructose, and says to adjust down when the absolute intensity is low. Jeukendrup wrote it while at the Gatorade Sports Science Institute (PepsiCo), which sells sports drinks.

A 2026 review, [Morton et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2026.101442), says the upper limit could rise from 90 to 120 g/h, at least for trained athletes, and that the 120 to 200 g/h some athletes now try isn't supported yet. Several of its authors have ties to Science in Sport and one to Nduranz, both sports nutrition companies.

## How much do ultrarunners actually eat?

Usually less than 90 g/h. A systematic review of 8 studies of one-day ultra-trail races, [Arribalzaga et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18115737), found most runners didn't reach 90 g/h, and six of the studies reported gut symptoms in 65 to 82% of runners.

Having a plan is linked to finishing faster. In a survey at the 2025 Western States 100, [Mougin et al. 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1440244026002318) found that 100% of finishers under 18 hours had a structured carb plan, against 69% of those finishing in 24 to 30 hours. It's self-reported, from one race, so it's a link, not proof that the plan made them faster.

## Is 120 g an hour better for an ultra?

Not proven. The only race trial is [Viribay et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12051367): elite male trail runners, all gut trained, ran a mountain marathon on 60, 90 or 120 g/h, with 6 or 7 runners per group. A marker of muscle damage (CK) rose 155.9% by the next day at 120 g/h, against 963.4% at 90 g/h and 976.2% at 60 g/h. Race time didn't differ. 6 of the 26 starters didn't finish, 3 of them for gut problems. Viribay is also a co-author of the Arribalzaga review.

In the lab, more carbs held late power better. In [Norte et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70326), 16 trained cyclists and triathletes rode 3 hours, then did a short all-out test. Critical power was 277 W fresh, and after the ride 236 W with water, 257 W with 60 g/h and 266 W with 120 g/h. Small, almost all men, no race time.

<!-- fig:fig2-late-power -->

There's a cost. In [Ravikanti et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00665.2025), 8 elite male marathoners used more of the carbs they drank at 120 g/h, but nausea, fullness and cramps peaked at that dose. Science in Sport, which sells carb products, funded it. An expert review, [Plews et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02462-z), notes that in one study the share of carbs used fell from about 86% at 90 g/h to about 76% at 120 g/h, leaving roughly 30 g/h unused. The authors say recommending more than 90 g/h to amateurs is premature. It's an opinion piece, and we haven't checked its funding statement yet.

## Why does the gut matter so much in ultras?

Because it ends races. At the Western States 100, [Stuempfle & Hoffman 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2015.1012104) surveyed 272 runners after the race. 96.0% had at least one gut symptom, mostly mild. But 43.9% of finishers said gut symptoms hurt their performance, and 35.6% of those who dropped out said gut symptoms were one reason, most often nausea. In 497 endurance athletes, [Mauvieux et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18071033) found gut symptoms in a race were the strongest factor linked to dropping out, and gut trouble in training was linked to gut trouble on race day. Both are self-reported surveys.

## Does heat change the math?

It may. In [Podlogar et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP289304), 17 endurance-trained adults rode 90 minutes at a low intensity taking 90 g/h of glucose. At 40°C (104°F) they used about 27% less of it than at 20°C (68°F): 0.37 against 0.51 g a minute. After 9 days of heat acclimation it stayed low, at 0.38. It's a lab study with glucose only, read from the abstract. A university news release links the senior author to a commercial carb test.

## What it means for your race

**Ultra.** A 50K that takes 5 hours and a 100-miler that takes 30 are different jobs: lower intensity can mean a lower target, and many hours of eating make the gut the limit. For a hot race, practice your fueling in the heat. Our article on [how long heat acclimation takes](https://raceproof.ai/blog/heat-acclimation-how-long) covers the heat block.

**Marathon.** Your finish time sets the target. Our [marathon carbs article](https://raceproof.ai/blog/carbs-per-hour-marathon) covers it.

**Triathlon.** A full distance means ultra-length eating. No study here tested 120 g/h across a whole triathlon.

**When to start.** Gut training has its own window: start about 12 weeks out, reach race-level carbs by 4 to 6 weeks out, then use your race fueling in every long session. [See your own timeline: try the demo](https://raceproof.ai/demo).

## Our take

*This is our opinion, not a study finding.* For most ultrarunners, a written plan you've practiced matters more than a big number. Keep 90 g/h as the default ceiling. Go above it only once 90 g/h has felt fine in 3 long sessions, add about 10 g/h a week, drop back 10 g/h after any session with moderate gut symptoms, and stop at 120 g/h. Those steps are our proposed rule, not something a study tested, and coaches still have to confirm them.

<!-- faq -->
### Do I need the same carbs per hour in a 50K and a 100-miler?
Not necessarily. [Jeukendrup 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-014-0148-z) says to adjust down when the absolute intensity is low, as in much of a 100-miler. What your gut tolerates over many hours matters as much.

### Is 120 g/h safe to try?
It has only been tested in trained or elite athletes, and gut symptoms peaked at that dose in [Ravikanti et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00665.2025). Build up slowly in training. If you have diabetes, ongoing gut problems or another medical condition, talk to your doctor before changing how you fuel.

### Why do I feel sick late in an ultra?
Nausea was the symptom most tied to dropping out at Western States ([Stuempfle & Hoffman 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2015.1012104)), and it peaked in the hardest, hottest part of the course. If nausea or vomiting won't settle during a race, stop and see the race medical team.

### Can I train my gut for an ultra?
Yes. Gut trouble in training is linked to gut trouble in races ([Mauvieux et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18071033)), so practice race fueling on long runs and fix problems before race day.
