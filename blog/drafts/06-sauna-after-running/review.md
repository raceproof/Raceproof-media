<!-- title -->
Does a sauna after running help?

<!-- answer -->
Probably a little, but the studies are small. In 6 male runners, 3 weeks of sauna straight after training let them run 32% longer to exhaustion, which the authors equate to about a 1.9% faster time trial. Hot baths after runs made a 5 km test in the heat 4.9% faster, with no change in cool conditions. Sauna is an optional extra, not a must, and it isn't safe for everyone.

<!-- body -->
## What happened when runners used a sauna after training?

The main sauna trial is tiny. In [Scoon et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2006.06.009), 6 competitive male distance runners did two 3-week blocks of normal training, with a 3-week break between them. In one block they added a humid sauna at about 90°C (194°F) for about 31 minutes straight after training, 12.7 sessions on average. One to two days after each block, they ran on a treadmill at 5 km race pace until they couldn't hold it.

After the sauna block, they ran 32% longer (90% confidence limits 21 to 43%). That sounds huge, but a run to exhaustion stretches small changes. The authors estimate it equals about a 1.9% (1.3 to 2.4%) gain in an endurance time trial. That 1.9% is their estimate, not something they measured.

<!-- fig:fig1-sauna-trial -->

Some popular posts say this trial proves sauna works. Six men in one crossover trial don't prove much on their own.

## Why could a sauna make you faster?

The likely reason is more blood. In Scoon's runners, plasma volume (the liquid part of blood) rose 7.1% (5.6 to 8.7%). Red cell volume rose 3.5%, but with a range from minus 0.8% to plus 8.1%, so that change is unclear. Longer runs went with bigger plasma volume gains (a correlation of 0.96), but that's a correlation in 6 people, not proof of the mechanism.

A study in cyclists points the same way. In [Stanley et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-014-3060-1), 7 well-trained men added 30 minutes of sauna at 87°C (189°F) and 11% humidity after training for 10 days. Plasma volume peaked after four sessions, 17.8% above baseline, and waking heart rate fell 10.2%. It had no control group and no performance test, and used a debated statistics method.

Hot baths after training seem to work the same way. In [Jenkins et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1113/jp289874), 10 well-trained runners (9 men) added five 45-minute baths a week at 40°C (104°F) or hotter for 5 weeks, and compared that with a time-matched control period. VO2max rose 2.7 mL/kg/min (95% CI 1.4 to 4.1), speed at VO2max rose 0.8 km/h, blood volume rose 284 mL and haemoglobin mass rose 33 g. Haemoglobin mass was the strongest predictor of the VO2max gain. Small sample, mostly men.

## Does it prepare you for a hot race?

Hot baths did in one trial, and only in the heat. In a randomized controlled trial, [Zurawlew et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12638), 17 men not used to heat ran 40 minutes a day for 6 days, then sat for 40 minutes in water at 40°C (10 men) or 34°C (93°F, 7 men). The hot-bath group's 5 km treadmill time trial in 33°C (91°F) improved 4.9%. At 18°C (64°F) it didn't change.

<!-- fig:fig2-passive-heat-studies -->

Passive heat is an add-on to heat training, not a replacement. The expert consensus, [Racinais et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094915), says acclimatisation should come from repeated exercise in the heat over 1 to 2 weeks. In a 7-session block of running at 40°C, 10 women and 8 men adapted the same way ([Keefe et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.90119.2026)). Our article on [how long heat acclimation takes](https://raceproof.ai/blog/heat-acclimation-how-long) covers that block.

## How long do the gains last?

Nobody tested it for sauna. Scoon's runners were tested 1 to 2 days after the block. For heat adaptations in general, a meta-analysis, [Daanen et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0808-x), found about 2.5% of the adaptations are lost for every day without heat, mostly in lab studies. So time it for the final weeks and keep some heat going.

## Is a sauna safe for everyone?

No. If you have a heart condition, high or low blood pressure, take blood pressure medication, are pregnant, or have ever fainted or had heat illness, talk to your doctor before you try a sauna or hot baths. For everyone else: after training only, well hydrated, never alone and never after alcohol.

## What it means for your race

**Marathon.** If your race is usually cool, a sauna is optional. Scoon's runners gained in a cool lab test, but that's one small trial. If your race is usually hot, put training in the heat first and use the sauna on top.

**Ultra.** Hot ultras reward heat work. In a survey at the 2025 Western States 100, [Mougin et al. 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1440244026002318) found 90% of runners prepared for the heat, and over 70% reported more than 10 days or exposures. It's self-reported, from one race.

**Triathlon.** A sauna after a bike or run session counts as heat time. Stanley's cyclists showed the plasma volume change within four sessions.

**When to start.** Heat has its own window: 6 to 2 weeks before your race, building over 10 to 14 days, then some heat to race day. These are general guidelines. [See your own timeline: try the demo](https://raceproof.ai/demo).

## Our take

*This is our opinion, not a study finding.* A sauna or hot bath after training is a fair optional tool. Expect a small gain, not 32%. For a hot race, Raceproof counts it as heat time, the same as a hot session, but never requires it. Build up slowly: for hot baths, our rule starts around 20 minutes and works toward 40 to 45 minutes at about 40°C, 3 to 5 times a week, an approximate number our coaches still have to confirm. Using sauna earlier in your build only for aerobic gains is still an open question for us.

<!-- faq -->
### Is a sauna as good as running in the heat?
The studies don't compare them directly. The expert consensus builds heat acclimatisation from repeated exercise in the heat over 1 to 2 weeks ([Racinais et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094915)). Sauna and hot baths add heat on top of that.

### How long should I sit in the sauna?
The studies used about 30 minutes: 31 minutes at about 90°C in [Scoon et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2006.06.009) and 30 minutes at 87°C in [Stanley et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-014-3060-1). Both studied trained men. Start shorter and leave if you feel dizzy or unwell.

### Does a hot bath work like a sauna?
In small studies, hot baths after training raised blood volume and VO2max over 5 weeks ([Jenkins et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1113/jp289874)) and improved a 5 km test in the heat after 6 days ([Zurawlew et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12638)). No study here compares the two directly.

### Does it work the same for women?
The sauna and bath studies can't say: they tested 6 men, 7 men, 17 men and 10 runners with 1 woman. With exercise in the heat, women and men adapted the same way ([Keefe et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.90119.2026)). If you're pregnant, talk to your doctor first.
