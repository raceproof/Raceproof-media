How do I avoid hitting the wall in a marathon?

<!-- answer -->
Start near your goal pace, not faster, and fuel from the start. In 78,912 Boston finishers, 26.6% of those who started more than 5% faster than their average pace hit the wall, against 1.5% of those who started near it. Most runners also under-fuel: in Seville, marathoners averaged 35 g of carbs an hour, while guidelines go up to 90 g/h for efforts over 2.5 to 3 hours.

<!-- body -->
## How common is hitting the wall?

Very common. [Smyth 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0251513) analyzed more than 4 million marathon race records from 270 races. He defined the wall as slowing at least 25% below your pace from 5 to 20 km, for 5 km or more after the 20 km mark. 28% of men and 17% of women hit it. When it happened, it started on average at 29.6 km for men and 29.3 km for women, lasted 10.72 and 9.61 km, and cost about 31.50 and 33.20 minutes against a recent best.

It was also more common in the years when runners were chasing a best time: 36% hit the wall in the 3 years before a recent PB, against 23% in earlier years.

This is observational race data. The wall is read from 5 km splits, so slowing from injury or under-training counts too, and the time cost is measured against an estimated best, not what the runner would have run that day.

## Does starting too fast cause the wall?

It's strongly linked to it. [Dong & Li 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0352808) sorted the split times of 78,912 Boston Marathon finishers (2015 to 2017) into pacing patterns. Runners who started more than 5% faster than their average pace hit the wall (here, slowing more than 20% at the end) 26.6% of the time. Runners who started near their average pace: 1.5%. Among even pacers it was 0.2%; among runners whose pace swung up and down, 85.4%.

Even pacing also went with faster finishes. Among 3:30 to 4:30 runners, even pacers finished 13.2 minutes faster than runners who slowed a lot in the second half. 82.0% of sub-3:00 runners paced evenly, against 44.4% of 3:30 to 4:30 runners and 19.6% of those over 4:30.

The limits matter. This is observational, so even pacing may be a sign of fitness rather than the reason for speed. It covers Boston qualifiers on one net-downhill course, with no training or weather data.

## Why does running out of carbs make you hit the wall?

Your muscles and liver store a limited amount of carbohydrate. A computer model of marathon fuel use, [Rapoport 2010](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000960), shows that the point where you run low depends on your pace as a share of your aerobic capacity, how much glycogen you start with and your muscle mass. Run slower or take carbs mid-race, and that point moves later. It's a model, not measured runners, but it explains why pacing and fueling are the same problem.

## Are most runners fueling enough?

No. In a survey of 160 runners at the 2022 Seville Marathon, [Jiménez-Alfageme et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-024-00801-w) found an average of 35 g of carbs an hour. 41.3% took under 30 g/h, and only 9.0% reached the 60 to 90 g/h range. Slower runners under-fueled more: 66.7% of those over 4 hours took under 30 g/h. It's self-reported intake, mostly from men.

The [2016 joint position statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26917108/) of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and the American College of Sports Medicine sets up to 90 g/h for exercise lasting more than 2.5 to 3 hours. Our article on [carbs per hour in a marathon](https://raceproof.ai/blog/carbs-per-hour-marathon) covers how to set your own target. Your gut can learn to take more: in a small controlled trial, 2 weeks of practice cut gut symptoms by about 60% ([Costa et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2016-0453)).

## Do more carbs protect your late-race pace?

In trained athletes in the lab, yes. In [Norte et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70326), 16 trained cyclists and triathletes rode 3 hours, then did a short all-out test. Critical power was 277 W fresh. After the ride it was 236 W with water, 257 W with 60 g/h and 266 W with 120 g/h. In [Mazza et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000004180), after 4 hours of riding, 15-minute time trial power fell 9.3% at 60 g/h and 6.4% at 100 g/h in 15 trained men.

Both are small controlled trials in cyclists, almost all men, and neither measured a marathon. They point the same way: fuel protects the end of the race.

## What it means for your race

**Marathon.** Pick a goal pace you've held in long runs, and run the first 10 km at that pace, not faster. Plan your carbs in grams per hour and practice them in every long run. If you have diabetes, ongoing gut problems or another medical condition, talk to your doctor before changing how you fuel.

**Ultra.** The studies above are marathons. In a survey at the 2025 Western States 100, [Mougin et al. 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1440244026002318) found that 100% of finishers under 18 hours had a structured carb plan, against 69% of those finishing in 24 to 30 hours. It's a self-reported survey at one race, so a link, not proof.

**Triathlon.** In a review of 70.3 studies, [von Känel-Cordoba et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0352506), bike speed fell 1.14 km/h in the second half, and conservative bike pacing that spares glycogen was linked to a better final run. Mostly observational data.

**When to start.** Goal-pace work counts from 10 weeks out. Gut training starts about 12 weeks out, with race-level carbs by 4 to 6 weeks out. Long sessions build across the whole 16-week block. These are general guidelines. [See your own timeline: try the demo](https://raceproof.ai/demo).

## Our take

*This is our opinion, not a study finding.* The wall is usually decided in the first 10 km and in the weeks before the race, not at km 30. Know your goal pace because you've held it on tired legs, not because a calculator gave it to you. Go out at that pace even when it feels too easy. And carry a fueling plan you've already practiced, in grams per hour.

<!-- faq -->
### At what km do runners hit the wall?
On average around km 29 to 30. In more than 4 million race records, it started at 29.6 km for men and 29.3 km for women ([Smyth 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0251513)).

### Is the wall the same as bonking?
Runners use both words for the same late-race collapse. In race data, the wall is a sharp, lasting slowdown late in the race. Running low on carbs is one main reason ([Rapoport 2010](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000960)), and a fast start is another strong link ([Dong & Li 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0352808)).

### Do women hit the wall less than men?
In race data, yes: 17% of women against 28% of men ([Smyth 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0251513)). The data can't say why.

### Can gut training help?
It helps you take in the carbs you plan. In [Costa et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2016-0453), 2 weeks of practice cut gut symptoms by 60% with gels and 63% with food in a small trial of 25 runners.
